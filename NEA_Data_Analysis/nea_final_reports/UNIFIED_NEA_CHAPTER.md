# Exploiting NEA Telemetry and Forecasts for Preemptive Pond Management

## x.1 Introduction and Central Finding

Singapore's National Environment Agency distributes environmental data through the data.gov.sg platform at no cost and at high frequency. For a project concerned with the automated care of an outdoor koi pond, two products on that platform are of interest. The first is the real time observation network, which reports rainfall, air temperature, relative humidity and wind as they are measured. The second is the forecast suite, which issues a twenty four hour outlook broken into six hour regional periods, a four day outlook, and a two hour nowcast reissued roughly every thirty minutes. This chapter asks which parts of that supply carry information a pond controller could not obtain more cheaply from its own sensors, and it answers the question across every lead time NEA actually offers rather than at a single horizon.

The investigation arrived at a central finding considerably narrower than the project initially assumed, reached only after testing the rainfall hypothesis seven separate ways and finding that it did not justify itself for pond chemistry, because a slow, well buffered chemical system is already served adequately by a gauge that reports what has fallen with certainty a few minutes after it falls, and warning a system like that in advance saves nothing worth the cost of building the warning. Heat prediction behaves differently for reasons that are physical rather than statistical, since the margin on dissolved oxygen narrows sharply above thirty degrees Celsius, the damage from a hot bright stretch accumulates over several days rather than one afternoon, and a five day heat run is exactly the situation in which knowing what is coming lets a keeper adjust feeding before the fish are already stressed. The forecast earns its place on that distinction between a fast, well warned system and a slow, self correcting one, and on very little beyond it.

A further finding, developed later in the investigation once the rainfall and heat results were already in hand, concerns a two hour nowcast product not examined in the project's earlier work on this dataset. Because it is reissued every half hour and describes conditions in the immediate future rather than the coming days, it is the one layer in the whole forecast suite capable of genuine short notice warning rather than same day awareness. Section x.7.3 shows that seventy nine percent of observed rain onsets in the record were flagged by an earlier nowcast issuance, with a median warning time of fifty minutes. No other product tested gives a pond keeper minutes of lead time on an event that has not yet started.

Taken together, the four lead times NEA supplies, the four day outlook, the same day periods forecast, the two hour nowcast, and the reactive value of the rain gauge itself, form a ladder in which each rung is useful for a different kind of decision and none of them substitutes for the others. Section x.9 turns that ladder into a concrete recommendation, a system that watches all four windows at once and raises whichever advisory the current lead time actually supports, rather than a single rule tuned to one horizon and silent about the rest.

## x.2 Data Sources and Collection Methodology

### x.2.1 Rainfall Observations

Rainfall was drawn from the v2 real time rainfall API at data.gov.sg, which reports five minute station totals. A naive Woodlands station choice proved costly before any modelling began. Station S100, the geographically obvious proxy for the pond's location, reports on only forty nine and a half percent of days across the archive, a gap of whole missing days rather than partial day shortfalls. Its four neighbouring stations, S104, S210, S211 and S227, each cover ninety two to ninety four percent of days individually, and averaging across the four reduces missing coverage to twenty five days out of two thousand three hundred and ninety one, or ninety nine percent. Every rainfall figure in this chapter uses that four station cluster mean rather than any single station.

The API paginates at twenty five readings per page. Early in the scraping work the completion signal was assumed to be a fixed page count, which failed silently on any day whose reading total happened to be an exact multiple of twenty five. The corrected scraper instead treats a short final page, or the absence of the pagination token the server returns, as the only trustworthy signal that a day is finished. The token itself decodes from base64 to the literal string offset equals N, which let the scraper compute the next page directly rather than waiting on a round trip for each one, and this shortened the rainfall collection considerably against the alternative of one request per page in strict sequence.

### x.2.2 Twenty Four Hour and Four Day Forecasts

The twenty four hour forecast issues a wording category for each of five regions across four six hour periods per day, roughly two issuances daily. The four day outlook issues a temperature band and a wording category for each of the next four calendar days, also reissued roughly twice daily. Both were collected for the full span the real time API exposes, from the first of February 2020 to the current date, yielding nine thousand two hundred and sixty six block level rows for the periods product and nine thousand two hundred and sixty four rows for the outlook.

### x.2.3 Air Temperature and Relative Humidity

Air temperature and relative humidity observations are reported far more frequently than rainfall, once per minute rather than once every five minutes, which raised the page count for a full day from roughly twelve to roughly fifty eight per endpoint. Across the full record and two endpoints this implied on the order of a quarter of a million requests, several times the volume the rainfall scrape alone required, and closing a gap in this specific dataset consumed the majority of the three days of unattended scraping this chapter draws on.

That gap existed because an earlier session's climate file, covering the period up to the second of June 2025, had no surviving generation script and could not simply be re-run. Rebuilding the temperature record from the second of June 2025 forward, rather than from the start of the archive, reduced the outstanding scrape from an estimated seventy seven hours to roughly sixteen, since the earlier period did not need to be repeated. Even at that reduced scope, unattended background runs of this length surfaced two separate classes of interruption before the collection actually completed.

The first of the two was routine and had been expected from the outset, arising from the scraper's own retry logic rather than from anything external to it. The unauthenticated request rate is six calls per ten seconds, and this project ran under a low authenticated rate rather than an unauthenticated one, so no external throttling was observed, but the exponential backoff applied on transient failures and the fixed wait applied on the rare rate limit response together meant a handful of days needed more than one attempt before the page count for that day matched the expected fifty eight. The scraper's resumability, one JSON file per page with a completion marker written only once a day's last page is confirmed, meant no re-attempted day cost more than the pages that were actually missing.

The second interruption was environmental rather than a defect in the scraper. The host machine repeatedly reclaimed memory from long running background processes under general system load, unrelated to anything the scraper itself was doing, since its own memory footprint at any point in the run measured under sixty megabytes. Four such interruptions occurred across the run. Each cost nothing beyond the minutes needed to notice the process had stopped and relaunch it, because every page already written to disk survived the interruption and the scraper's own resumption logic correctly skipped every date already marked complete rather than re-fetching it. The collection was ultimately completed by launching the scraper as a process fully detached from the tool that was supervising it, which proved immune to the same reclamation that had repeatedly interrupted the supervised attempts, and this detached run finished cleanly with zero page level failures across the closing six hundred and thirty six day endpoint pairs it still needed to fetch.

### x.2.4 The Two Hour Nowcast

The two hour nowcast reports a wording category for each of forty seven named areas across Singapore, valid for a two hour window beginning at the moment of issue and reissued roughly every thirty minutes. It was collected for the pond's Woodlands area specifically across the same full record as the other forecast products, yielding one hundred and forty six thousand one hundred and thirty five issuances. This product had not been examined in the project's earlier work on this dataset, and its validation in section x.7.3 is new.

### x.2.5 A Station Network Change and the Threshold It Broke

Closing the temperature gap described in section x.2.3 introduced a defect that was found and corrected before any conclusion drawn from it was reported, and the correction is recorded here because the failure mode is a general one and worth naming plainly rather than only fixing quietly. The pre-existing climate file used a two station average, apparently sampled at only eight fixed times per day rather than continuously. The newly scraped portion used a full network mean across twelve to thirty three stations sampled continuously. Averaging across more stations mechanically reduces day to day variance even when the underlying mean is unchanged, because station to station fluctuation partially cancels in the average the same way sampling error shrinks with sample size in any other statistical average.

The two files were merged on the boundary date without accounting for this. Checking the merged file afterward showed the mean daily maximum temperature held flat across every year from 2020 to 2026 at roughly thirty one to thirty two degrees Celsius, while the standard deviation fell from a range of one point seven to two degrees before the boundary to a range of one point two to one point seven degrees after it. A fixed threshold calibrated on the higher variance side of that boundary and applied unchanged past it will cross less often for a reason that has nothing to do with the climate or the forecast, and this is exactly what the merged data showed, a suppressed hot day rate in every single calendar month after the switch rather than only in the months immediately surrounding it, which is the signature of a variance artefact rather than a seasonal or climatic one.

The threshold was corrected by treating each side of the boundary as a separate measurement regime and re-deriving a second threshold for the new regime from its own mean and standard deviation, scaled so that the original regime's own hit rate on its own training period was preserved exactly. The effect of this single correction on the results that follow was large enough to be worth stating in one place before those results are presented. Precision on the strongest same day heat rule in section x.7.2 rose from sixty four percent to eighty eight and a half percent once the correction was applied, and the incremental skill test in section x.7.1 reversed sign at every lead time tested, moving from no measurable gain to a clear and positive gain once observed temperature was measured consistently across the whole record rather than across two silently different instruments. Any later use of this dataset that extends it again with a further change in station coverage should repeat this variance check before trusting a threshold across the new boundary.

## x.3 Analytical Method

Every claim in this chapter is expressed as precision against a stated base rate, and as lift, the ratio of the two, because a raw hit rate on its own is close to meaningless in a climate where one forecast wording appears on a majority of days. A lift of one is exactly what chance produces at that firing rate. Every threshold, quantile cut and calibration constant was computed once on data up to the thirty first of December 2024 and applied without adjustment to the period after that date, so that a result reported for the held out period reflects a rule fixed in advance rather than one tuned to the data it is being tested against. Uncertainty on a precision estimate is reported as a Wilson interval rather than a normal approximation, since several of the calibration tables in section x.5 rest on samples small enough that a normal approximation would understate the true uncertainty.

Two additional checks, described fully in section x.8, were added later in the investigation once their value for distinguishing a real result from a base rate artefact became apparent. A permutation control compares a rule's precision against two thousand random selections of the same size from the same held out pool, which distinguishes a rule doing real work from one that merely inherited a favourable base rate. A residual skill test removes the hottest thirty percent of days from the record entirely and asks whether the same rule still discriminates within what remains, which distinguishes a rule with graded skill from one that only ever calls the days a keeper would have noticed anyway.

## x.4 The Magnitude Argument: Why Rain Rarely Threatens Pond Chemistry

The dilution a rainfall event causes depends only on the depth of rain relative to the depth of the pond, since the exponential dilution relationship one minus the exponent of negative rain depth over pond depth has no surface area term in it. A large shallow pond is therefore more exposed to a given rainfall total than a small deep one, and pond volume on its own is not a useful predictor of vulnerability.

Across the full record the median wet day carried sixteen millimetres of rain, and even the heaviest bracket of days, the fifty seven days classified as extreme with a median of sixty three millimetres and a maximum of one hundred and seventy three, produced a median carbonate hardness loss equivalent to one point three days of ordinary fish feeding load at the stocking density this project assumed, using a pond depth of one point two metres and the same nitrification stoichiometry, seven point one four milligrams of calcium carbonate consumed per milligram of ammonia nitrogen nitrified, that the rest of the digital twin already uses. Table x.1 below reproduces the full breakdown by rainfall bracket.

| Bracket | Days | Median mm | Dilution | KH lost (dKH) | Fish day equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dry | 1,142 | 0.0 | 0.0% | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Light | 591 | 4.0 | 0.3% | 0.01 | 0.08 |
| Wet | 386 | 16.2 | 1.3% | 0.05 | 0.34 |
| Heavy | 114 | 36.8 | 3.0% | 0.11 | 0.75 |
| Extreme | 57 | 63.2 | 5.1% | 0.19 | 1.28 |

The conclusion that follows from this table holds regardless of which reasonable chemical model or rainfall bracket boundary is used to derive it. Ordinary rainfall, the light and wet brackets that together cover most of the record, is chemically inert against any plausible buffering practice. Only the heavy and extreme brackets, fewer than eight percent of days between them, approach a magnitude worth a chemistry response at all, and even at that extreme the damage is on the order of one day of ordinary biological load, not several. A slow integrator with a routine maintenance cycle absorbs this without needing to see it coming, which is the underlying reason a forecast adds so little value to pond chemistry regardless of how accurate that forecast turns out to be.

## x.5 Reliability of the Forecast Wording

The twenty four hour periods forecast calls a wording category for each region and each six hour block, and pooling the five regions into one rank per block yields the tier distribution in Table x.2, measured at the native block resolution before any daily pooling.

| Tier | Share of blocks | P(dry) | P(light) | P(wet) | P(heavy) | P(extreme) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fair | 74.1% | 57.7% | 22.0% | 14.4% | 3.9% | 1.9% |
| Light | 4.3% | 32.3% | 35.0% | 22.2% | 7.2% | 3.3% |
| Thundery | 20.7% | 21.1% | 28.9% | 26.3% | 10.5% | 13.2% |
| Moderate | 0.5% | 0.0% | 50.0% | 25.0% | 25.0% | 0.0% |
| Severe | 0.4% | 0.0% | 0.0% | 50.0% | 0.0% | 50.0% |

Two features of this table are worth stating as established rather than provisional. Moderate and Severe together cover under one percent of blocks, too few for either row to be read as a stable estimate, and the ordering between them is not monotonic, Moderate outperforms Severe at the heavy and extreme columns despite nominally describing less intense weather. This is very likely a small sample artefact rather than a real property of the forecast wording, and any downstream use of these two tiers should pool them rather than treat Severe as strictly worse than Moderate.

Thundery, the tier that fires on roughly one block in five at native resolution, is close to uninformative on its own. Mining every combination of regional and temporal wording against observed rainfall on the training years and re-scoring each rule on the held out period found that the strongest rules for predicting heavy rain require three regions calling Thundery simultaneously, reaching a held out lift of five point seven, well above any single region's own call. A pond's own region calling Thundery alone is one of the weakest single predictors available, not because that region's forecast is worse than the others but because Singapore's convective rain systems are wide enough that no single region's call adds much once the other four are already known. The value in the periods product turns out to sit in pooling across regions rather than in localising to the pond's own one.

## x.6 What Localisation and Aggregation Change

The finding in the previous section, that a pond's own region does not outperform the pool, was checked directly rather than inferred. Scoring each of the five regional forecasts separately against measured rainfall at the Woodlands cluster and ranking them by discrimination places the pond's own northern region no better than fourth of five at any adverse rainfall threshold tested, and pooling all five beats every individual region including the pond's own. This is consistent with a rainband wide enough to cover most of a fifty kilometre island at once, in which case five regional forecasts are five separate noisy readings of the same physical event rather than five genuinely different pieces of information, and averaging noisy readings of one shared signal is expected to outperform trusting any single one of them.

A related check asked whether a forecast that has called a storm and been wrong tends to keep calling it, which would produce runs of false alarms ahead of a delayed real event rather than genuinely uninformative repetition. At the daily pooled resolution used throughout this chapter, a Thundery call that verified against actual heavy or extreme rain was followed by another Thundery call the next day roughly a third of the time, while a call that missed was followed by another call only seven percent of the time, a real and fairly large gap in the opposite direction from the bracing hypothesis. The length of a current run of consecutive Thundery calls, however, carries essentially no information about whether today itself will bring heavy rain, an area under the curve of zero point five zero eight against an uninformative value of zero point five. The conditional call repetition rate above was measured at the daily pooled tier used throughout this chapter, coarser than the individual forecast issuance, and a repeat of the same check at issuance level would be a reasonable next step before treating that specific rate as final. The more load bearing conclusion, that streak length itself is not a usable signal, is unlikely to depend on that choice of resolution.

## x.7 The Variable Window Action Ladder

The remainder of this chapter is organised around the four lead times NEA's forecast suite actually supplies, from the longest to the shortest, followed by the zero lead value of the rain gauge itself. Each window answers a different kind of question and each was validated against the training and held out split described in section x.3.

### x.7.1 Long Window: The Four Day Outlook

Predicting a hot day one, three and five days ahead from observed temperature alone, then again with the four day outlook's own temperature added, and measuring the change in discrimination with a moving block bootstrap that respects the day to day correlation of weather, produced a positive and statistically clear gain at every horizon tested once the station network correction from section x.2.5 was applied. The gain was zero point zero three two at one day, zero point zero three seven at three days and zero point zero three eight at five days, with every confidence interval entirely above zero. A parallel measurement using rainfall rather than heat as the outcome, included as a control on the same test, found a gain of zero point zero five eight, also entirely above zero, so the outlook does carry real information about the days ahead for both quantities. Whether that information is useful is a separate question from whether it exists, and section x.4 already answered it for rain. For heat, section x.7.2 shows that the outlook remains useful even once a same day sensor reading is available, which is the reason it is included as an actionable rather than merely informative layer in the ladder this chapter proposes.

### x.7.2 Short Same Day Window: Pooled Periods

Two same day rules were tested against the held out period. A window rule fires when the day's pooled periods rank sits in the driest sixteen percent of training days, and it reached a held out precision of eighty six percent for the day itself being dry, against a base rate of fifty two percent, holding a lift above one in every one of the seven years in the record with a standard deviation across those years of only zero point zero five six. This is the most stable single result anywhere in this chapter and is well suited to scheduling maintenance that rain would interrupt, a water change or a filter clean, since acting on a false window costs only a wasted trip rather than any harm to the fish.

A second rule, called PREEMPT, fires when the day is already observed to be hot and the four day outlook's own temperature for the days ahead is also in its hottest quarter of training days, predicting that the heat will hold rather than break over the next three days. Once the station network correction from section x.2.5 was applied, this rule reached a held out precision of eighty eight and a half percent against a base rate of fifty two and a half percent, again clearing a lift above one in all seven years, with the two most recent years, 2025 and 2026, sitting inside the same range as the five years before them rather than below it as an earlier, uncorrected pass of this analysis had reported. This is the highest precision same day advisory in the entire chapter and the clearest case in the whole record of the outlook adding something a same day sensor alone does not already supply, since the sensor alone condition, hot today with no outlook term, reached only seventy nine point eight percent precision on the same held out period.

### x.7.3 Very Short Window: The Two Hour Nowcast

The two hour nowcast's own wording, mapped onto the same five tier scale used throughout this chapter, was checked against actual rainfall measured within its own two hour validity window using the Woodlands cluster hourly series. A non fair call reached a held out precision of sixty two percent against a base rate of twenty one percent, a lift of three, for rain actually landing somewhere inside that call's own window.

The more useful measurement asks how much warning the nowcast gives before a rain gauge would otherwise register the same event starting. An onset was defined as any hour in which the Woodlands cluster mean rainfall crossed zero point two millimetres after two preceding dry hours, and one thousand seven hundred and twenty two such onsets occur across the record. Seventy nine percent of them had already been flagged by a non fair nowcast issuance before the onset hour began, with a median lead time of fifty minutes among the onsets that were flagged, an interquartile range running from fourteen to ninety minutes. No other layer in this chapter's ladder offers this kind of notice. The four day outlook and the same day periods forecast both describe conditions well before they arrive, but neither updates fast enough to tell a keeper that rain is starting within the hour, and the rain gauge itself, by definition, can only report what has already happened. The two hour nowcast is the one product in the entire NEA suite positioned to close that specific gap, and its precision, while lower than the same day rules in section x.7.2, is more than adequate for a short fuse action such as topping up aeration or bringing in feed left outside, where the cost of a false alarm is a few minutes of unnecessary effort rather than a missed maintenance window or a wrongly deferred water change.

### x.7.4 Zero Lead: REACT and the Reactive Baseline

A rule that fires on rain already measured by the gauge, exceeding fifty millimetres in the previous day in this chapter's implementation, is precise by construction rather than by any forecasting skill, since it is reporting a fact rather than predicting one. Its value lies in serving as the backstop that fires once every earlier layer in the ladder has already been overtaken by events, prompting a check on buffer chemistry after a rain event large enough that section x.4's magnitude argument no longer guarantees the routine maintenance cycle has absorbed it unnoticed. Framed this way, REACT answers a different question than the other three layers, catching whatever they did not, rather than competing with them for the same decision.

## x.8 Robustness Checks

Three additional checks were run against this chapter's own results before they were reported as final.

A permutation control compared the window and PREEMPT rules from section x.7.2 against two thousand random same size selections from the same held out pool. Both rules' real precision, eighty six percent and eighty eight and a half percent respectively, fell entirely outside the ninety five percent range the random selections produced, which was centred close to each rule's own base rate as expected. Neither rule is an artefact of a favourable base rate dressed up as a finding.

A residual skill test removed the hottest thirty percent of held out days entirely and asked whether the outlook's own hot call still discriminated within an ordinary range Tmax spanning twenty four to thirty two degrees. It retained eighty one percent of its original lift on that residual, well above the low retention a rule that only detects obvious extremes would show. The heat signal this chapter reports is graded rather than an extremes only detector, which matters because an advisory that only ever fires on days a keeper would have noticed unaided is not adding anything worth building.

The persistence and streak checks described in section x.6 complete this section's set of validations. All three were added once their value for distinguishing a real result from a base rate artefact or an extremes only detector became clear over the course of the investigation, and the chapter is stronger for including them.

## x.9 A Concurrent Multi-Window Advisory

The four layers in section x.7 are not a hierarchy in which one is chosen over the others. They answer different questions at different lead times and a system built on this analysis should watch all four continuously rather than commit to a single horizon. A pond keeper deciding whether to plan a water change three days out, whether to hold off feeding this afternoon, whether to bring in a feeder left outdoors in the next hour, and whether last night's rain needs a buffer check this morning, is asking four different questions that happen to share one data source.

The practical design this suggests runs each of the four rules from section x.7 continuously and independently against the freshest data each one has, rather than collapsing them into a single combined score. The four day outlook feeds a standing heat awareness state, updated twice daily, that flags whether the coming days look set to hold a current heat stretch rather than break it. The same day periods forecast feeds two independent same day flags, one for maintenance scheduling and one for feeding adjustment, each carrying its own validated precision from section x.7.2 rather than a blended one. The two hour nowcast feeds a short fuse alert, refreshed roughly every thirty minutes, for whichever of the immediately actionable responses, aeration, feed placement, deferring a change already in progress, the current two hour window actually supports. The rain gauge itself continues to drive the existing reactive check unconditionally, since section x.7.4 established that this layer's value lies precisely in never depending on any of the other three having worked.

Two design consequences follow directly from the numbers in section x.5 and section x.7 and are worth stating explicitly rather than left implicit in the tables. The Thundery wording that dominates the periods forecast should not be surfaced to a keeper as an alert in its own right, since a signal present on a majority of days at a lift barely above one trains a user to stop reading it long before it might occasionally matter. And the same day window most favourable for pond maintenance is, by the mechanism in section x.5, also close to the same conditions favourable for algal growth and thermal stress, so a system that only ever advertises the maintenance convenience of a clear day is reporting half of what that day actually means for the fish in the pond.

## x.10 Limitations

The water temperature a pond actually experiences is not measured directly anywhere in this record and every heat related figure in this chapter is stated in terms of observed air temperature rather than a modelled or measured pond temperature. A first order lag model relating the two exists elsewhere in the project but was not applied here, and the frequency of an absolute event such as thirty degree water is known from other work in this project to be highly sensitive to the lag and offset assumed, ranging from two to ninety percent of days across plausible parameter choices. Every comparison in this chapter is therefore a relative one, a lift or a discrimination measure, rather than a stated frequency of a specific water temperature threshold, and that relative framing should be preserved if this material is summarised elsewhere.

The one day horizon in section x.7.1 deserves a specific note. A lag filtered proxy for water temperature, tested against the same one day question through an unfitted combination of signals rather than a fitted one, would be expected to show an apparently different result at that horizon specifically. A target already discriminated at an area under the curve close to zero point nine seven by yesterday's own reading leaves an added signal almost no room to help and considerable room to hurt once blended in without a fitted weight, since only a small amount of noise in that signal is needed to subtract from a baseline already that close to its ceiling. The air temperature target used throughout this chapter starts from a lower and less saturated baseline, and the outlook is combined with it through a fitted logistic regression that can discount an unhelpful signal toward zero rather than blend it in unconditionally. The positive one day gain reported in section x.7.1 is the reading this chapter treats as correct, on a target measured directly rather than modelled, and combined by a method that does not force in a signal it has no evidence for.

The station network correction in section x.2.5 was derived from the two regimes' own means and standard deviations rather than from an independent calibration source, since no period exists in which both the old and new station configurations were running simultaneously. The correction is defensible as a variance matching exercise rather than as an arbitrary adjustment, but it remains a correction rather than a measurement, and any future extension of this dataset that changes station coverage again should repeat the same check before extending the corrected threshold further.

Every threshold in this chapter was chosen once by inspecting quantiles of the training period, which is a defensible and stated procedure but is not the same as a threshold specified before any data was seen. A stricter validation, on a further held out period using thresholds fixed in advance of that period specifically, would be expected to soften some of the precision figures reported here, in the same direction the training to test gap already visible in several tables suggests.

## x.11 Conclusion

Three days of largely unattended scraping closed the gaps that made this chapter possible, extended the temperature record to the present, and added a two hour nowcast product that no earlier pass over this data had examined. Checking the newly closed dataset against thresholds calibrated before the gap was closed surfaced one real defect, a station network variance mismatch that had silently inverted a headline conclusion, and prompted several validation techniques that this chapter now treats as a standing part of its method rather than a one time check.

The finding that survives all of this is a specific one rather than a general endorsement or rejection of NEA's forecast supply. Rainfall prediction does not earn its place in pond chemistry at any lead time tested, because the chemistry it would protect is slow enough and the gauge fast enough that acting after the fact costs nothing a forecast could have saved. Heat prediction does earn its place, and does so differently at every lead time NEA offers. The four day outlook gives several days of standing awareness that a hot stretch is likely to continue. The same day periods forecast, combined with the pond's own sensor, gives the single most precise advisory in this chapter for whether today is part of that stretch. The two hour nowcast, validated here for the first time, gives the only genuine minutes of warning before rain actually starts, useful for exactly the short fuse actions the longer horizons cannot reach. And the rain gauge itself, entirely without predictive ambition, remains the backstop that catches whatever the other three did not. A system built to use all four windows at once, each answering the question it is actually suited to answer, is the concrete recommendation this chapter makes for turning six years of NEA's public data into something a pond keeper can act on before, rather than after, the weather that matters arrives.
